What Coaches Want to See

  • Plays that translate to their level: speed, physicality, technique, and football IQ.
  • Consistent effort, not just one splash play.
  • Clear evidence of your role responsibilities within the scheme.
  • Situational value: third down, red zone, special teams, and late-game reps.

Film Length

  • Aim for a concise highlight: typically 3 to 5 minutes.
  • If you have more strong clips, create position-focused versions rather than one long video.
  • Keep full-game film available separately for deeper evaluation.
  • Update throughout the season instead of waiting for year-end.

Best Plays First

  • Your first 20 to 30 seconds should show your highest-level traits immediately.
  • Open with 4 to 6 clips that define you as a player.
  • Include variety early: different alignments, situations, and competition levels.
  • Do not bury your best reps at the end.

Position-Specific Examples

  • QB: pocket movement, decision speed, accuracy by level, and third-down execution.
  • WR/TE: release vs press, route pacing, separation, contested catches, and run-after-catch.
  • RB: vision, contact balance, pass protection reps, and burst through tight windows.
  • OL/DL/LB/DB: hand usage, leverage, pursuit effort, and finish through whistle.

Common Mistakes

  • Too many low-impact clips and not enough true evaluation reps.
  • No pre-snap indicator or spotlight for where you line up.
  • Overuse of dramatic effects, long intros, or heavy music distractions.
  • Outdated film that does not represent your current size, speed, or role.

When to Contact Coaches

  • Send updates after meaningful events: strong game film, improved test numbers, or GPA wins.
  • Reach out ahead of key windows: camps, visits, and dead/evaluation periods.
  • Avoid random messages with no new value.
  • Be consistent enough to stay visible but not so frequent that messages feel repetitive.

Email Template Structure

  • Subject line: name, class year, position, school.
  • Opening: short intro and one-line reason for contacting that specific program.
  • Middle: one update plus film link and core measurables.
  • Close: one clear ask, such as camp attendance or feedback request.
  • Signature: full name, phone, email, high school, coach contact, and profile link.

Texting Etiquette

  • Keep messages brief and easy to answer.
  • Use proper grammar and punctuation; avoid slang-heavy messages.
  • Do not text repeatedly if there is no reply yet.
  • Text at reasonable times in the coach's time zone.

Calling Coaches

  • Prepare three talking points before you call: update, fit question, and next step.
  • Practice a 20-second introduction so you sound confident and clear.
  • If you get voicemail, leave a concise message and follow up once by text or email.
  • After calls, log notes and action items so you can follow up accurately.

Social Media Best Practices

  • Pin key recruiting posts: latest film, measurables, and contact info.
  • Keep posts aligned with your brand as a disciplined student-athlete.
  • Never post content that could raise maturity or character concerns.
  • Engage respectfully when interacting with programs and staff.

Following Up Without Overdoing It

  • If no response, wait and follow up with a real update, not just checking in.
  • Space messages based on recruiting calendar and relationship strength.
  • Use one communication lane at a time before switching channels.
  • Always keep your tone professional, patient, and respectful.

Which Camps Matter?

  • Prioritize camps where schools you already communicate with will actually evaluate your position.
  • Focus on quality over quantity: a few strong performances beat a packed but unfocused schedule.
  • Use camp choices to answer your current recruiting question: exposure, validation, or offer movement.
  • Avoid events that do not provide position-specific testing or coach interaction.

Prospect Camps vs. Showcases

  • Prospect camps usually involve direct coaching by college staffs and can impact board placement quickly.
  • Showcases can provide broad exposure and testing, but direct school-specific feedback may be limited.
  • If your goal is an offer from School X, their camp often matters more than a general event.
  • Use showcases to create initial awareness, then follow up with targeted school camps.

How to Prepare

  • Train for the exact drills you will perform: starts, transitions, and position movement.
  • Plan hydration, sleep, and nutrition 48 hours before camp day.
  • Arrive with a short self-introduction and your recruiting profile links ready.
  • Set one performance goal and one relationship goal before each event.

What to Bring

  • Cleats, backup cleats, gloves, and weather-appropriate gear.
  • Water, electrolyte options, and simple fuel snacks.
  • Notebook or notes app for coach feedback and next steps.
  • Printable profile sheet or digital QR link for easy follow-up.

Following Up Afterward

  • Send a short thank-you message within 24 hours.
  • Reference one specific drill or coaching point to show attention to detail.
  • Share updated film when you apply their feedback in games or workouts.
  • Ask one clear next-step question: visit, call, or camp return date.

When a Camp Is Worth the Investment

  • It has direct access to coaches on your target list.
  • You are healthy and prepared to perform near your best.
  • The event timing supports your recruiting window, not just your schedule.
  • You can clearly explain what outcome you expect from attending.

Official vs. Unofficial Visits

  • Official visits are school-funded and usually happen later in recruiting. Use these for final fit decisions.
  • Unofficial visits are self-funded and can happen earlier. Use them to gather early information and build relationships.
  • On any visit, ask for your realistic role and timeline, not just what sounds good in the moment.
  • If possible, visit during both a game weekend and a normal school week so you see both versions of campus life.

Questions to Ask Coaches

  • Where exactly do you project me in your scheme and depth chart over the next 2 years?
  • What specific skills or metrics do I need to improve to earn early playing time?
  • How do you develop players at my position week to week?
  • How stable is the staff, and who will coach my room day to day?
  • What does off-season training and recovery look like for my position group?

What Coaches Are Evaluating

  • How you interact with everyone: staff, players, hosts, and support workers.
  • Body language during meetings, campus tours, and downtime.
  • How well your personality fits the locker room and culture.
  • Whether your family communication style matches the program environment.
  • Your maturity: punctuality, respect, and ability to ask thoughtful questions.

Campus Visit Checklist

  • Tour football spaces: locker room, training room, weight room, meeting rooms, and recovery options.
  • Tour academics: tutoring center, major department, class buildings, and advisor support.
  • Meet current players in your position room and ask what daily life is really like.
  • Review distance from home, travel logistics, weather, and housing.
  • Write notes right after the visit before details fade.

Red Flags to Watch For

  • Different staff members give conflicting messages about your role or scholarship status.
  • Current players quietly warn you about communication or trust issues.
  • No clear development plan at your position.
  • Pressure to commit immediately before you finish comparisons.
  • Promises are made verbally but never documented or explained in detail.

Academic Fit

  • Major/field fit: what school or field are you most interested in?
  • Admission path: do you need separate application/acceptance into that college (for example, business or communications)?
  • Weekly schedule reality: how many classes per day and what does a typical week look like in-season and off-season?
  • Class/credit structure: are there limits on classes you can take, and are options online, in-person, or both?
  • Graduation roadmap: how many credits are required, and are credits transferable if your path changes?
  • Help/tutors: what tutoring access is available, where is it located, and what does the study hall/program actually include?
  • Career development: what formal career development programming exists for athletes?
  • Academic support and graduation outcomes: ask about graduation rates and post-college placement support.

Facilities

  • Do the day-to-day facilities help you become the best player on and off the field?
  • Can you train and develop effectively and efficiently in those spaces?
  • Do you personally like the environment and feel comfortable there?
  • Locker room: you will spend major time here, so make sure it fits your needs and comfort.
  • Practice fields/indoor: can you master your craft day or night; evaluate surface quality, weather options, and training tools (bags, jugs, etc.).
  • Weight room: check rack availability and whether there is adequate indoor speed-training area.
  • Training/recovery room: does it include complete recovery resources (hydro, float tanks, cryo, nap/sleep area, sauna/steam, etc.)?
  • Position meeting rooms: are they spacious, updated, and useful for your specific position group?
  • Player lounge: another space you will use often, so it should support comfort and reset time.
  • Nutrition hubs/stations: one or multiple, self-serve or staffed, and do they offer what you need?
  • Study center: is it convenient and easy to use consistently?
  • Parking: understand athlete parking costs, availability, and fine/ticket policies.

Coaching

  • Coaching/support staff: are they genuine, and do you and your family feel comfortable around them?
  • Head coach: is he established at the university, and what is his stability (contract years / hot-seat risk)?
  • Coordinator/position coach: does the scheme maximize your chance of success?
  • Ask for details on their tendencies: run/pass ratio, man/zone ratio, and blitz usage.
  • Ask where they project you positionally (for example: WR X/W/Z, DT nose/1-tech/3-tech).
  • Strength and conditioning: 1-on-1 vs group work, staff-to-player ratio, and summer/spring plan details.
  • Ask if they are using modern sports-science methods and recovery/training best practices.

Exposure

  • What is the program's short- and long-term plan for player promotion?
  • Are there new promotion opportunities or activities in motion?
  • Do they have a specific exposure plan for you, with examples from current top players?
  • What does their branding program include for athletes?
  • What media expectations exist, and what opportunities are available to build your brand?
  • Do they provide social-media education/development programming (group sessions or individual)?
  • Ask for direct feedback on your current brand: what is strong, what is weak, and what to improve now and later.

Support Staff

  • You may spend as much (or more) time with support staff as with coaches, so evaluate this group carefully.
  • Equipment staff: attitude and daily interactions matter; respectful relationships here pay off.
  • Nutrition staff: ask about nutritionists/chefs/serving support and whether communication is athlete-centered.
  • Strength assistants: who works with your position group, and what extra development/recovery help do they provide?
  • Medical team: understand trainer access and doctor network coverage for injuries and long-term care.
  • Operations/player development/front office/security: ask how they support athletes and families during problems.

Traditions

  • What traditions define the program (gameday, kickoff windows, bands/tailgates/rivalry atmosphere)?
  • What is unique about this program compared to peer schools?
  • Where does the program stand within its conference today?
  • Which program tradition impressed you most, and why?
  • Can you and your family genuinely see yourselves enjoying that gameday environment?

Football Opportunity

  • Depth chart and realistic path to playing time.
  • Scheme fit for your position and skillset.
  • Coaching staff stability and development history.

Environment & Lifestyle

  • Distance from home and support network.
  • Campus culture and athlete expectations.
  • City, weather, and day-to-day comfort.

Financial & Future

  • Scholarship details and out-of-pocket cost.
  • NIL opportunity and support structure.
  • Network value beyond football.
